How CLIP is trained: a matching game, played 32,768 wide

CLIP never sees a label. It sees a batch of images and a batch of captions and is asked one question: which goes with which? This post takes that question apart — the two encoders, the similarity matrix, the symmetric loss, the temperature knob — and then lets you train a miniature CLIP in your browser and watch the embedding space organise itself.

Notice what made it hard. Two of the images share a subject, so the only way to tell their captions apart was the detail — the beach, the harness. Those near-misses are called hard negatives, and they are where almost all the learning signal comes from. Now imagine the same game with 32,768 images and 32,768 captions at once. That single round is one training step of CLIP.

The whole idea in one sentence. Train an image encoder and a text encoder so that, inside a batch, every image scores highest against its own caption and every caption scores highest against its own image. Everything else on this page is the machinery that turns that sentence into gradients.

2 · Two encoders, one space

CLIP is two separate networks that never talk to each other until the very end. The image tower turns pixels into a vector; the text tower turns a caption into a vector of the same length. Each vector is then pushed onto the unit sphere. Hover a box for what CLIP actually used.

Why normalise? Because length is a cheat code.

Without normalisation the loss could be gamed by just making vectors longer — a huge vector dotted with anything gives a huge score. Projecting onto the sphere leaves only direction, so the only way to score well is to point the same way as your partner. Toggle it below.

Three image vectors (teal) and three text vectors (rose) in a 2-D joint space drag any point

Raw dot products are dominated by whichever vector is longest: I1 points almost exactly along the short T2, yet the long T1 wins the raw score. Stretch T2 and the raw winner flips without any change of direction. On the sphere, only the angle survives.

3 · The batch is the whole dataset, for one step

Take N image–caption pairs, embed them all, and dot every image against every caption. You get an N×N grid of cosine similarities. The N cells on the diagonal are the true pairs — the positives. The other N²−N cells are negatives, and CLIP never has to go looking for them: the batch supplies them for free.

The grid below is real. A miniature CLIP — two tiny encoders over a toy world of coloured shapes and short captions — was trained in this tab when the page loaded. Hover any cell. Switch to the untrained model to see what the same grid looks like before a single gradient step.

Why the batch size is the hyper-parameter that matters. Two encoder passes per pair buy N²−N comparisons. At N = 8 there are 56 negatives; at CLIP's N = 32,768 there are about 1.07 billion per step. More negatives means a harder game per step and, crucially, a much better chance that the batch contains a near-miss — another photo of a dog, another caption about a beach. Section 6 lets you measure this. Look also for cells marked as false negatives: two pairs of the same concept in one batch are still scored as non-matches. CLIP does nothing about those; with 400M pairs it simply does not care.

The formula behind the code

Lij = exp(t) · cos(Ii, Tj)   scaled similarity matrix, N × N
lossi→t = −(1/N) Σi log exp(Lii) / Σj exp(Lij)   each image picks its caption
losst→i = −(1/N) Σj log exp(Ljj) / Σi exp(Lij)   each caption picks its image
loss = ½ (lossi→t + losst→i)
∂loss / ∂Lij = ½ (Prowij − δij) / N + ½ (Pcolij − δij) / N   step 8: the gradient matrix, P − I

This is the InfoNCE loss of van den Oord et al., applied twice and averaged. Two things are worth keeping. First, it is a plain N-way classification: for image i, the "classes" are the N captions in the batch and the answer is caption i. Second, the gradient on every logit is simply probability minus label: each negative is pushed down in proportion to the probability the model gave it, and the positive is pulled up by 1 − p. Negatives the model already dismisses contribute almost nothing. The hard negatives carry the step — push one up in the grid above and watch both its row and its column pay for it.

Where the numbers come from. The paper traces the objective from Sohn's multi-class N-pair loss (2016) through InfoNCE (2018) to ConVIRT (2020), which applied it to medical image–report pairs. CLIP's contribution was not the loss. It was the discovery that this loss, at web scale, produces a general-purpose visual model.

5 · Temperature: the knob CLIP learns for itself

Cosines live in [−1, 1], and in a trained CLIP they live in a much narrower band than that: matching pairs score around 0.3, unrelated pairs around 0.1 to 0.2. Feed numbers like that straight into a softmax over 32,768 candidates and the probabilities are almost uniform, so the loss barely moves however good the geometry is. CLIP multiplies every cosine by a scale exp(t) before the softmax — equivalently, divides by a temperature τ = 1/exp(t). The paper initialises τ at 0.07 (borrowed from Wu et al. 2018), learns it by gradient descent, and clips the scale at 100 because larger values made training unstable.

One image's row of eight cosines, in the narrow band a trained CLIP actually produces: the true pair at 0.31, the closest non-match at 0.24, the rest down to 0.08. Every number above is computed from these eight values.

Three regimes. At τ = 1 the softmax is nearly flat: the positive gets barely more probability than anything else and the gradient is spread thinly across every negative. Worse, because cosines cannot exceed 1, even a perfect geometry cannot get the loss below ln(1 + (N−1)e−2/τ) — at τ = 1 and N = 32,768 that floor is 8.4. Around 0.07 the hardest negatives dominate the gradient, which is what you want. Push towards 0.01 and the loss becomes a contest between the positive and the single closest negative, with everything else ignored — and a single negative that overtakes the positive sends the loss past 10, which is why the scale is clipped. The released CLIP checkpoints sit exactly at that clip: their logits are cosine × 100, i.e. τ = 0.01. The miniature model in the next section drifts the same way, and you can watch it happen.

6 · The lab: train CLIP in your browser

Everything above, running for real. The world is nine concepts: three colours × three shapes. An "image" is a 12-number feature vector (colour, shape, size, position, noise) drawn as an icon. A "caption" is a bag of words from a 14-word vocabulary, generated from templates like "a photo of a red circle" or "the square is blue". Two small MLP encoders map both into a 2-dimensional space, so after normalisation every embedding is a point on a circle and you can see the whole space at once. The loss is the symmetric cross-entropy from Section 4, the temperature is learnable and clipped at a scale of 100, and the gradients are computed by hand-written backprop — checked against finite differences to 10−8.

What each experiment shows

  • 1 · Default run. At step 0 the nine captions sit almost on top of each other and the images occupy their own arc: two random networks map their inputs into two narrow cones (the dashed arrows are the mean embedding of each modality — long means concentrated). Watch the captions fan out first, then the image clusters drift to meet them, then τ fall as the softmax sharpens. The loss never reaches the amber floor because a batch of 16 drawn from 9 concepts almost always contains two pairs of the same concept — false negatives, marked amber in the heatmap.
  • 2 · τ frozen at 1. The clusters still form, but the loss stalls high: with cosines capped at 1, a flat softmax cannot get below the floor shown in amber. This is the reason CLIP learns τ instead of fixing it.
  • 3 · Tiny batch. With N = 4 a batch usually contains no same-colour or same-shape near-miss, so separating one attribute already wins most rounds. Watch the colour and shape bars separate, and notice how long it takes for the second attribute to be learned at all.
  • 4 · SigLIP. Every cell becomes its own yes/no question with a learned bias b initialised at −10, so the 240 negatives start out already "correct". No chance line is drawn because there is no softmax to be uniform over. The clusters form just the same; the difference is memory, not geometry.
  • 5 · Collisions. N = 32 over 9 concepts means every batch has several duplicates. Count the amber cells: those are pairs the objective insists on pushing apart even though they are the same concept. CLIP never masks them; at 400 million captions the collision rate is simply low enough not to matter.
What the toy cannot tell you. Real CLIP embeds into 768 dimensions, not 2; its text encoder is a Transformer that can see word order; and its two modalities stay in separate cones after training — Liang et al. (2022) measure the distance between the image and text centres of a trained CLIP at 0.82, and show that the low temperature is what keeps them apart. On a circle there is no room for two cones once nine clusters have to spread out, so here they dissolve. Occasionally a run also gets stuck with two clusters in the wrong order — on a one-dimensional ring, clusters cannot pass through each other. That trap does not exist in 768 dimensions; press Reset.

7 · The classifier that writes itself

After training, the text encoder has become something strange: a machine that turns a sentence into a classifier weight vector. To classify an image into K classes, embed K prompts, embed the image, take K cosines, scale by exp(t), softmax. No fine-tuning, no labels. The paper found the wording of the prompt matters — "A photo of a {label}." beat the bare label by 1.3 points on ImageNet, and ensembling 80 such templates added another 3.5 — because a full sentence looks more like the captions the model was trained on. The toy reproduces the mechanism: its training captions were always sentences, so a bare "red circle" is out of distribution for it too.

The trick generalises far beyond classification. Retrieval is the same computation with the roles swapped: embed a query sentence, rank a million image embeddings by cosine. Stable Diffusion conditions its denoiser on CLIP text embeddings; LLaVA feeds CLIP's ViT-L/14 patch features into a language model. In every case the asset is the shared space, not the classifier.

8 · What 400 million pairs actually costs

CLIP's dataset, WIT, was built by searching for 500,000 queries (every word appearing at least 100 times in English Wikipedia, plus bigrams, article titles and WordNet synsets) and keeping up to 20,000 pairs per query. Every model was trained for 32 epochs at a batch of 32,768. Move the sliders to see what those choices imply.

The engineering that made a 32,768 batch fit

  • Sharded similarity. No GPU ever holds the full N×N matrix — the pseudocode's single np.dot hides this. Each GPU computes only the pairwise similarities its local slice of the batch needs, against the gathered embeddings from every other GPU.
  • Mixed precision, gradient checkpointing, half-precision Adam statistics, and text-encoder weights stored in half precision with stochastic rounding.
  • Adam with decoupled weight decay (0.2), 2,000 warm-up iterations, cosine decay. β₂ = 0.98 for the ViTs rather than the usual 0.999.
  • Compute. The largest ResNet (RN50x64) took 18 days on 592 V100s; ViT-L/14 took 12 days on 256 V100s — about and V100-days respectively. The similarity matrix is not where that time goes: over the whole run it is about 4.2 × 1014 dot products of 768 numbers, while the two encoders process 12.8 billion images and captions. The encoders dominate.

Why contrastive at all? The efficiency argument

OpenAI's first attempt predicted captions word by word, like an image-captioning model. It worked, but slowly. The paper reports two efficiency ratios measured by the rate at which zero-shot ImageNet accuracy improved with data:

Relative efficiency as stated in Section 2.3 and Figure 2 of the paper: a bag-of-words predictor learns 3× faster than the transformer language model, and the contrastive objective a further 4× faster than bag-of-words. The curves themselves are not reproduced here.

The reason it is faster. Predicting the exact words of a caption forces the model to spend capacity on everything that is unpredictable about web text — phrasing, typos, usernames. Predicting only which caption goes with the image lets the model ignore all of that and keep what is shared between the picture and the words.

9 · What the objective never asks for

A model learns exactly what the loss rewards and nothing more. Every well-known CLIP failure is a feature the matching game did not require.

Word order

If no caption in the batch is a re-ordering of another, the model is never punished for treating text as a bag of words. Yuksekgonul et al. (2023) built the ARO benchmark from exactly such pairs: asked to pick the right caption out of two that differ only in a relation or attribute, CLIP scored 59% and 62% against a chance level of 50%. Adding word-order-shuffled captions to each batch as hard negatives (NegCLIP) lifted the relation score to 81%. Our toy's text encoder is a bag of words by construction — try it:

The modality gap

In a trained CLIP, image and text embeddings occupy two separate regions of the sphere. Liang et al. (2022) trace it to the cone effect: a random deep network maps all its inputs into a narrow cone, the two towers start in two different cones, and the contrastive loss at a low temperature has a repulsive structure that keeps them apart — the measured gap of 0.82 is a loss minimum, and closing it raises the loss.

In the lab right now: cone concentration at init — images —, captions — (1 = a single point); positive-pair cosine — at init → — now.

Whatever the captions never mention

The image tower keeps only what helps pick the right caption. The paper lists the casualties: counting objects, fine-grained classes (car models, aircraft variants, flower species), and handwritten digits — zero-shot CLIP reached only 88% on MNIST, below a logistic regression on raw pixels, because almost nothing in web captions looks like a handwritten "7".

The lab measures the same mechanism: changing only an image's size, a feature no caption mentions, moved its embedding by — at init and moves it by — now.

Typographic attacks

Because the text tower learned to read words in images (rendered text is cheap contrastive signal), Goh et al. (2021) found that a Granny Smith apple with a paper label reading "iPod" is classified as an iPod. The objective rewarded reading the label exactly as much as recognising the fruit.

10 · PM lens — what to ask before you ship a CLIP-like model

You will meet CLIP-style models as an image-search feature, a content-moderation classifier, an embedding API, or the vision half of a multimodal assistant. The questions below follow directly from how it is trained.

Six questions

  • "What was it trained on, and can we say so?" — The objective needs hundreds of millions of web-scraped pairs. WIT was never released; LAION was built from Common Crawl and has been withdrawn and re-issued over content concerns. Provenance is a product risk, not a research footnote.
  • "Zero-shot on whose prompts?" — Section 7 showed a template change moves accuracy. Any zero-shot number you are quoted is a number for one prompt set; ask for the ensemble, and for the prompts.
  • "Is the task a matching task?" — Retrieval, tagging, de-duplication and coarse classification are matching tasks and CLIP excels. Counting, reading small text, relational questions ("left of", "holding") are not, and a captioning or VLM model will do better.
  • "What is the recall at the k we actually show?" — For search, evaluate with Recall@k and NDCG on your own queries (Post 65), not with ImageNet accuracy from the model card.
  • "Softmax or sigmoid, and what batch?" — If you are training or fine-tuning, the batch size is the negative set. Small-batch fine-tuning of a CLIP model with the softmax loss is a common way to quietly degrade it; SigLIP's loss is the safer default at small scale.
  • "Has anyone tried the typographic attack?" — A moderation classifier that reads text in images can be steered by text in images. Red-team it with labels before someone else does.
The single most useful mental model. A CLIP embedding answers "what caption would the web have put under this?" — not "what is in this image?" The two overlap enough to be useful and diverge enough to bite. Every failure mode in Section 9 is a place where the web's captions and the truth disagree.

  • A uniform guess scores ln N. With N = 32,768 that is 10.4; and with cosines bounded, the best possible loss at temperature τ is ln(1 + (N−1)e−2/τ) — 8.4 at τ = 1, essentially 0 at 0.07. That is why τ is learned.
