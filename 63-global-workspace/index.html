🧠 What is Global Workspace Theory?

Neuroscientists have long proposed that the brain has a global workspace — a shared "broadcast" system where specialized processors (vision, language, memory) compete to send information to a central hub. That hub then broadcasts the winning signal back out, enabling conscious, flexible, coordinated behavior.

This paper asks: Do large language models develop an analogous structure? The answer is yes — and it's measurable.

Core Finding: Claude's intermediate layers contain sparse, word-like representations that carry ~88% of the model's causal reasoning load — while accounting for only ~10% of activation variance. These are the "unspoken words" Claude reasons with internally.

🔬 The Jacobian Lens (J-Lens)

To study which internal representations actually cause outputs (not just correlate with them), Anthropic introduces the Jacobian Lens: a linearized causal probe that measures how much each hidden layer activation influences the final output token probabilities.

Jℓ = 𝔼[∂hfinal,t' / ∂hℓ,t]

Where h_ℓ,t is the activation at layer ℓ, position t, and h_final,t' is the final residual before the unembedding. The expected Jacobian gives us a ranked vocabulary list — the J-lens readout — representing which tokens are most causally upstream of the output at that layer.

Key insight: Unlike probes (which only detect information), the J-lens reveals information that is actually used to produce outputs. Layer 50 might contain everything — but if its J-lens readout is empty, that layer isn't contributing causally.

Example: Given "What is 8 - 2?", the J-lens at middle layers shows "six", "6", "answer" — before the model has generated any output. These are the model's internal "thoughts".

Technical note: Computing exact Jacobians is expensive. The paper uses structured approximations (Lanczos iteration + randomized SVD) that make the method tractable at scale. The readout is computed as a sparse combination of top vocabulary directions.

✨ J-Space: The Hidden Vocabulary

Not all J-lens vectors are equally informative. Across many inputs and layers, a sparse subspace emerges — the J-space — spanned by at most 25 basis vectors per layer. This is Claude's internal vocabulary of concepts.

J-space representations are:

  • Interpretable: Each basis vector aligns with recognizable English tokens (e.g., "spider", "mammal", "six")
  • Causal: Swapping a J-space vector from one computation into another changes the model's behavior predictably
  • Sparse: Any given token uses only ~3-8 of the 25 basis vectors at once
  • Non-negative: The combination weights are always positive — no canceling interference
Why non-negative? This constraint (NMF — non-negative matrix factorization) is crucial. Positive-only combinations produce parts-based representations analogous to how humans think in terms of semantic parts (e.g., "spider" = "arachnid" + "legs" + "web"), not arbitrary vector algebra.

⚗️ Five Functional Properties

The paper tests whether Claude's J-space actually plays the five roles predicted by Global Workspace Theory. Click each property to expand details and experimental evidence.

1. Verbal Report — "The model can say what it knows"
88%
▶

If J-space encodes what Claude "knows" internally, swapping J-space vectors between positions should redirect what the model reports. Success rate: 88% across diverse tasks (arithmetic, factual recall, multi-hop reasoning).

Example: During "how many legs does a spider have?" → J-lens reads "eight", "arachnid", "legs" at workspace layers. Swapping this representation with the "octopus" computation context causes the model to say "eight" (octopus arms) — wrong domain, right number transfer.

Intervention: replace J-space[spider] → J-space[octopus] → output shifts from "eight legs" to "eight arms" context ✓
2. Directed Modulation — "Goals reshape processing"
Top-Down
▶

In a global workspace, high-level goals can selectively amplify or suppress lower-level processing. The researchers test this by injecting goal-relevant J-space vectors at workspace layers and measuring whether early sensory representations shift accordingly.

Result: injecting "translate to French" into the workspace changes how Spanish words are processed in sensory layers — a genuine top-down control signal, not just early-layer filtering.

Goal injection at L60 changes phonological processing at L20 for the same token — causally upstream influence
3. Internal Reasoning — "Serial thought in J-space"
Multi-hop
▶

Multi-hop reasoning (e.g., "What is the capital of the country whose river runs through Cairo?") requires intermediate results. The J-lens traces this: at early workspace layers, "Egypt" appears; at middle layers, "Africa" and "Nile" emerge; at late workspace layers, "Cairo", "capital" dominate.

This is internal deliberation — sequential concept activation within a single forward pass, resembling a "scratchpad" that never surfaces in outputs.

Cairo → [workspace]→ Egypt → Africa → Nile → "The Nile" (final answer) — all in J-space, invisible to output
4. Flexible Generalization — "Same mechanism, different tasks"
76/192
▶

The paper tests 192 diverse task pairs: does the same J-space intervention work across completely different tasks? In 76 cases (40%), the same structural "swap" succeeds across different domains (math→language, factual→creative).

This is the hallmark of a truly general workspace — not task-specific circuits, but a shared representational substrate.

J-space swap from arithmetic context successfully redirects poetry completion task — 76 out of 192 domain-crossing pairs work ✓
5. Selectivity — "Automatic vs. deliberate tasks"
Gated
▶

In human cognition, automatic tasks (recognizing a word) bypass the global workspace; deliberate tasks (solving a puzzle) engage it. The same holds for Claude: J-space activations are much sparser during overlearned completions ("The sky is ____") vs. genuine reasoning tasks.

Ablating J-space representation hurts complex reasoning far more than simple pattern-completion, confirming the workspace is selectively recruited.

Simple: "Paris is the capital of ___" → J-space barely activates. Hard: "If A=B and B=C and C=?" → rich J-space activity ↑

🛡️ Safety Applications

The J-space framework opens powerful new tools for AI alignment and safety. Two immediate applications:

🔍 Alignment Auditing

Read the J-lens during a conversation to see what concepts are causally active in the workspace — even if they never appear in the output.

Example: A model asked to "help plan a negotiation" might show "leverage", "manipulation", "exploit" in J-space while outputting polite, neutral text. The J-lens catches hidden intent.

Hidden intent detection

🌱 Counterfactual Reflection Training

Training technique: ask the model "what ethical concepts were present in your reasoning?" — then fine-tune to populate J-space with ethical concepts like "fairness", "harm", "consent" during decisions.

Result: After CRT, J-space naturally surfaces "manipulation" and "exploitation" as warning signals, causing the model to re-route its reasoning.

Values-aligned reasoning

📊 Interpretability Audits

J-space provides a human-readable window into model reasoning at every layer — not just final outputs. Auditors can trace which concepts drive which decisions.

This is the first interpretability method that is both causal and lexically interpretable at scale.

Compliance-ready

🔧 Targeted Steering

Rather than steering via prompts (which affect all layers), J-space interventions can selectively modify workspace representations mid-computation.

This allows precise correction of reasoning errors without disrupting the model's overall capabilities.

Surgical correction

⚠️ Critical Perspectives

Important limitations and open questions to consider:

  • The J-space decomposition assumes linearity. Real transformer dynamics are highly nonlinear — the linearized Jacobian may miss important interactions (superposition, attention nonlinearities).
  • Results are primarily on Claude Sonnet 4.5. It's unclear if the same structure emerges in smaller models, heavily fine-tuned models, or fundamentally different architectures (e.g., Mamba, mixture-of-experts).
  • The 88% causal-load figure comes from swapping experiments — correlation with behavior ≠ complete mechanistic understanding. The remaining 12% may contain crucial edge cases.
  • Alignment auditing via J-space requires real-time Jacobian computation during inference — currently expensive. Practical deployment needs ~100× speedup.
  • The "Global Workspace" framing is provocative — it doesn't imply consciousness. The functional analogy is real; claims about subjective experience are not supported.
  • Training signal matters: a model fine-tuned to produce misleading J-space readouts could defeat auditing. Adversarial robustness of J-space is untested.
