What does NDCG actually measure?

Every ranking metric answers one question: how much did the order cost you? NDCG answers it by giving each result a gain, shrinking that gain by how far down the page it sits, and dividing by the best you could possibly have done. Drag the results below and watch the number move.

1 · The problem no set-based metric can see

Here are two result lists for the same query. They contain exactly the same eight documents — same relevant ones, same junk. Only the order differs.

Precision@5 and Recall are identical. Only the order-aware metric separates them.

Precision, recall, F1 and hit-rate all treat a result list as a set. Shuffle the set and they never flinch. But a user reads top-down and stops early, so ranking A and ranking B are not remotely equally useful. NDCG exists to price that difference.

Two ideas, one metric. NDCG bakes in exactly two beliefs: (1) a very relevant document is worth more than a marginally relevant one — that's the gain; (2) a document buried at rank 9 is worth less than the same document at rank 1 — that's the discount. Everything else in the formula is bookkeeping.

4 · Why log₂, and what happens if you change it

The discount is the whole reason NDCG is a ranking metric. The standard choice is 1 / log₂(i + 1): position 1 keeps 100% of its gain, position 2 keeps 63%, position 10 keeps 29%. It decays — but gently, so results at rank 20 still count for something.

Weight retained at each position

Changing this rewires the lab above — scroll back and watch NDCG move.

Pick 1 and you have destroyed the metric: with no discount, DCG is just a sum over the top-k, so every permutation of the same k documents scores identically. Pick 1/i and rank 10 retains only 10% instead of 29% — the metric now cares almost exclusively about the first three slots.

The honest answer. The log discount is not derived from user behaviour data; Järvelin and Kekäläinen chose it in 2002 because it decays smoothly and its exact base cancels out during normalization. If your users genuinely never scroll past rank 3, a harsher discount models them better — just say so explicitly rather than quietly reporting "NDCG".

5 · Linear gain vs. exponential gain

The second dial is how much a grade-3 document is worth relative to a grade-1. Two conventions are in common use, and papers frequently fail to say which one they used.

Under the exponential form one grade-3 hit outweighs seven grade-1 hits. Under the linear form it outweighs three.

This is a reproducibility trap. The same ranking, same judgments and same k can yield materially different NDCG under the two gain functions — and the two are rarely labelled. sklearn.metrics.ndcg_score defaults to linear; TREC tooling, LightGBM and most learning-to-rank literature use exponential. Before you compare your number to a published one, confirm you are computing the same metric.

6 · Not all mistakes cost the same

Take your current ranking and swap any two positions. The grid below shows what that swap does to NDCG@k — rose means the swap makes things worse, teal means it makes things better, and grey means NDCG cannot tell the difference.

ΔNDCG@k from swapping position i with position j

Hover (or tap) a cell to read the exact change.

Two structural facts fall straight out of this picture. First, the top-left corner is loud and the bottom-right is quiet: an error between ranks 1 and 2 costs far more than the same error between ranks 7 and 8, because the discount curve is steepest at the top. Second, entire regions are flat grey — swapping two documents with equal relevance grades, or two documents both sitting below the cutoff k, changes nothing at all.

Why this matters for model training. This asymmetry is precisely what LambdaRank and LambdaMART exploit: instead of a differentiable loss, they weight each pairwise swap by the |ΔNDCG| it would cause. The grid you are hovering over is the gradient signal.

7 · Four ways NDCG lies to you

Press each button. It reconfigures the lab into a state where the number stops meaning what you think it means, and the explanation updates to say why.

The averaging trap. NDCG is normalized per query, which is what makes queries comparable — but it also means an easy query with one relevant document and a hard query with forty both max out at 1.0. A mean NDCG of 0.82 across a query set tells you nothing about which queries are broken. Always report the distribution, not just the mean.

If your users see exactly one answer, MRR is a truer model of their experience. NDCG earns its complexity when you have real graded labels and users who scan several results.

9 · PM lens — what to actually ask for

You will meet NDCG in three places: a retrieval vendor's benchmark table, your own RAG evaluation dashboard, and a model card. In all three it is quoted as a single number, and a single number is exactly the thing this metric cannot honestly be reduced to.

Five questions that expose a bad NDCG claim

  • "What is k, and why?" — k should come from how many results a user actually sees. A vendor quoting NDCG@100 for a UI that shows three results is measuring something nobody experiences.
  • "Linear or exponential gain?" — section 5. If they cannot answer, their number is not comparable to anyone else's, including yours.
  • "Show me the distribution, not the mean." — a mean of 0.82 is consistent with "uniformly decent" and with "excellent on 80% of queries, catastrophic on the 20% that matter most." Ask for the 10th percentile.
  • "How were the judgments produced, and how complete is the pool?" — unjudged documents score as irrelevant (section 7), so a benchmark whose pool was built from an incumbent system's candidates is structurally biased against any challenger that retrieves differently.
  • "How many queries had zero relevant documents, and what did you do with them?" — the IDCG = 0 case has no agreed answer, and the choice moves the headline number.

When NDCG is the wrong instrument

NDCG earns its complexity only when you have graded judgments and users who scan multiple results. Two common situations where it is the wrong choice:

  • RAG retrieval feeding a fixed context window. If you always pass the top 8 chunks to the model, ordering within those 8 barely matters — the model reads all of them. What matters is whether the necessary chunk is in the set at all. Recall@8 is the honest metric; NDCG@8 will flatter you.
  • One-right-answer lookup. Navigational search, entity lookup, "which policy covers this" — the user wants one document. MRR models that experience directly and is far easier to explain to a stakeholder than a discounted, normalized, exponentially-gained sum.
The offline/online gap is the real risk. NDCG is an offline proxy built on judgments from raters who are not your users, guessing at intent they cannot see. Ranking changes that move NDCG by a point routinely fail to move click-through, and vice versa. Treat NDCG as a fast, cheap regression detector for the ranking pipeline — the thing that tells you a deploy broke something — and let online experiments decide what actually ships.

10 · The whole thing on one card

gain(rel) = 2rel − 1    or just    rel
disc(i) = 1 / log₂(i + 1)   i is 1-indexed
DCG@k = Σi=1..k gain(reli) · disc(i)
IDCG@k = DCG@k of the same documents sorted by relevance, descending
NDCG@k = DCG@k / IDCG@k   ∈ [0, 1]; define as 0 when IDCG = 0
  • 1.0 does not mean "good results" — it means "no achievable reordering of the judged pool would have been better."
  • NDCG@k only sees the top k. Documents at k+1 and beyond are invisible, however relevant.
  • Two implementations disagreeing usually differ on the gain function, or on how they handle an empty relevant set.
  • Report the per-query distribution alongside the mean, and always state k and the gain convention.

Original formulation: Järvelin & Kekäläinen, Cumulated Gain-Based Evaluation of IR Techniques, ACM TOIS 2002. Exponential gain and the ΔNDCG-weighted training objective come from the LambdaRank / LambdaMART line of work at Microsoft Research.
